# SY0-701 objective 3.1: Architecture models and security implications

[Security+ index](../../README.md) · Domain 3: Security Architecture

Original study notes aligned to the public CompTIA SY0-701 V7 blueprint; not official training or exam questions.

## Explain it

Cloud, on-premises and hybrid designs shift which party operates and secures each layer; a shared-responsibility matrix makes those boundaries explicit. Virtualization, containers, serverless and microservices can improve deployment and isolation but add orchestration, identity, image and interface risks. Infrastructure as code makes changes reproducible yet can propagate an insecure template at scale. Software-defined networking centralizes policy but its controller becomes a critical dependency.

Compare centralized with decentralized control, scalability, availability, resilience, cost and recovery—not merely whether a technology is fashionable. IoT, embedded devices, industrial-control/SCADA systems and real-time systems may have long lifecycles, safety constraints and limited patch windows. Physical isolation or an air gap reduces some remote paths but does not eliminate removable-media or maintenance risk.

## Deeper distinctions

Architecture is a tradeoff among responsibility, control, recoverability and cost. In SaaS the provider usually manages more of the stack than in IaaS, but the customer still owns identities, data governance and configurations assigned to it. A responsibility matrix names who does what and who verifies it. Hybrid deployments add trust boundaries, network routes and inconsistent policy. Centralized control aids consistency but can concentrate failure; decentralized control improves local autonomy but complicates oversight.

Containers share a host kernel and rely on image provenance, runtime limits and orchestrator policy. Serverless removes server administration from the customer's routine tasks, not application code and identity risk. IaC enables reviewable, repeatable builds; protect templates and pipeline credentials because one mistake can spread broadly. OT and embedded devices may be safety-critical and difficult to patch. Air gaps require control over maintenance laptops and removable media; a diagram alone is not proof of isolation.

**Design drill:** Compare a new cloud workload with an on-premises controller. Identify the boundary, owner, update path, monitoring source and plausible common-cause outage for each.

## Additional blueprint concepts

An architecture decision should document compute and power dependencies, patch availability, recovery effort and whether risk is actually transferred by contract. An outsourced provider can operate a control while the customer retains accountability for its data and access. High availability may rely on several instances yet share a single power feed or identity provider. In OT, a real-time operating system and safety constraints can make ordinary patch cycles impractical; isolation, maintenance windows and tested recovery become more important.

## Apply it

A plant controller cannot be patched during production. Segment it, restrict maintenance access, monitor protocol traffic, document recovery and coordinate changes with operations so safety and availability remain explicit.

## Active recall

For a cloud-hosted application, identify two customer-owned controls and two provider-owned controls; explain why a container is not a complete security boundary.
